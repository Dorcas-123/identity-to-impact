      What is Identity to Impact?

      Identity to Impact is a free toolkit that helps churches run hands-on science and building activities with young people aged 13–19. While they build, test, fail, and try again, young people also explore who God says they are, and how that can change the way they live and serve others.

      What does STEM mean?

      STEM stands for Science, Technology, Engineering, and Math. It is learning by doing: asking questions, designing, building, testing, and improving. Many activities also use drawing, storytelling, and creative design, because people need to see and share ideas before they can improve them.

      Why it exists

      • To reach young people in a practical way. Hands-on activities give churches a creative way to connect with young people in their communities.
      • To work anywhere. Many STEM programs need expensive equipment, technology, or experts. This toolkit uses everyday materials and works without electricity or internet.
      • To connect faith to real experience. In many Christian STEM programs, a Bible verse is added at the end. Here, what young people experience while building connects directly to who God made them to be.

      What young people gain

      • Practical skills: problem-solving, teamwork, and creativity.
      • Confidence to try again after something fails.
      • A clearer sense of who they are in God.
      • Ideas and courage to help their community.

      Who it’s for

      Church youth leaders, local facilitators, and short-term mission teams working with a local church. You don’t need to be a scientist or a pastor to use it. Activities are written for young people aged 13–19. Adapt challenges and discussion to your group’s experience, interests, and support needs.

      How it works

      Each session follows the same simple steps: a short big idea, a hands-on challenge, building and testing, improving the design, sharing, and reflecting. Reflection follows three steps: Unlearn the voices that have told us who we are, Rediscover who God says we are, and Live it out. Then it moves outward to Impact in the community.

      The heart behind it

      We don’t just want young people to build cool things. We want them to become people whose work blesses others.

      Anyone can learn to build something impressive. But every creation carries something of its maker. A cracked vessel can still hold water for a while, but it can’t carry it far. In the same way, what we build in the world is shaped by what’s happening within us: what we believe about ourselves, what drives us, and who we’re building for.

      Skills without a healthy sense of identity can be used to prove ourselves, to compete, or to hide our fears. Skills built on who God says we are can be used to serve, to try again after failure, and to lift up a community.

      That’s the journey from identity to impact: what happens on the inside shapes everything we put out into the world.

      What it costs

      The toolkit is free. Most materials are recycled or easy to find, and a few cost a little.

      Purpose and faith

      The big idea

      How we see ourselves shapes how we build, what we build, and how we live. Changing how we see ourselves isn’t just swapping bad thoughts for good Bible verses. It’s letting what we believe about God and ourselves change how we live.

      1. UnlearnNotice the voices that have told us who we are, and check them against what God says. These voices can be other people’s opinions, culture, our past, our successes, our failures, or our own thoughts.
      2. RediscoverGo back to the Bible to learn who God is and who he made us to be.
      3. Live it outLet what is true change our choices, our relationships, and how we live.
      4. ImpactA changed life spills over to others. Like one domino, one small act can start a chain, and God makes it go far.

      Why building? How we see ourselves shows up the moment we build. Someone who thinks “I’m bad at this” plays it safe, gives up after one failure, or lets others take over. Building follows the same three steps. A failed test shows what was wrong. The team finds what works. Then they build again differently.

      How faith fits

      • Each session starts with a short big idea. The Bible passage comes during Reflect, after young people have done the activity.
      • Use the Bible translation your church uses. Each concept has short notes on what its passage means and doesn’t mean.
      • Ask questions before you explain. Let young people make the connection first.
      • Young people from any background are welcome. No one is pressured to believe or to share.

      Change is God’s work

      Romans 12:2 says “be transformed.” It is something God does in us. That’s why young people are asked to “live it out,” not to change themselves by trying harder. This keeps faith from becoming a test they pass or fail.

      Involving families and leaders

      Young people don’t take part on their own. Families and church leaders decide whether young people can come, and whether their ideas are taken seriously afterward.

      • Introduce the program first. Before the first session, meet with the pastor and invite parents to a short welcome. Explain what young people will do, why it matters, and how they will be kept safe.
      • Keep families informed. Tell them what each session is about, so they can ask their young person about it at home.
      • Invite them in at the end. The showcase gives families and leaders a chance to see what young people made and to support their ideas.
      • Protect young people’s privacy. Families can see what young people build, but what young people share during reflection stays in the group. Explain this to parents at the start.

      Keeping everyone safe

      Four rules that protect young people

      1. A build result never measures a person. A broken egg doesn’t mean “you’re broken.” A fallen tower doesn’t mean weak faith.
      2. Unlearning means checking voices, not rejecting people. Much of what parents, elders, and culture teach is good and true. Never ask young people to name specific people.
      3. Talking about the past can bring up pain. If a young person shares abuse or harm, facilitators follow the steps in the facilitator section.
      4. No one has to share. Anyone can pass on any question.

      Safety and forms

      Most forms are optional. Three are required:

      • Consent from a parent or guardian, for every young person.
      • Health and emergency information for every young person, kept private.
      • A disclosure record, whenever a young person shares that they are being harmed.

      Before your first session, choose a safeguarding lead. Make sure every facilitator knows what to do if a young person shares that they are being harmed.

      Include everyone

      Young people with disabilities or learning differences should take full part, not just watch. You don’t need to be an expert.

      • Ask the young person what helps them take part, not only their family.
      • Give everyone a real role, and take turns leading.
      • Show, tell, and let young people touch. Let them answer by speaking, drawing, pointing, or acting.
      • Change the activity, not the goal.
      • Use respectful words. Never use someone’s disability as an example in a lesson.
      • Ask for local help: inclusive education teachers, sign language interpreters, and disability organizations. Local notes list contacts for some countries.

      After the sessions

      Keeping it going

      A program that depends on one person or one visit usually stops when they leave. Plan for it to continue from the start.

      • Train the next facilitator early. Use the handoff path in Training from the first session, not at the end.
      • Fit it into what already exists, such as a church youth group, Sunday school, or a school club, instead of running it as a separate event.
      • Keep a shared supply box that stays with the church, so materials don’t need to be collected again each time.
      • Set a regular time, such as the first Saturday of each month, so people know when it happens.
      • Check in after a few months. Is it still running? Who is leading it? What needs support?

      Leading a session

      For anyone leading a session. You don’t need to be a scientist, a pastor, or a disability expert.

      In short: before the session, gather materials and try the activity yourself. During the session, follow the six-step flow and keep safety and reflection. End with one small action for the week.

      Start here

      1. Collect consent and health forms for every participant.
      2. Choose a concept and one activity. Wonderfully made is a good first session.
      3. Try it yourself and gather materials. The calculator tells you how much.
      4. Run the six steps: Gather, Challenge and predict, Build and test, Redesign, Share, Reflect.
      5. Follow up: each young person tells a partner their Live it out plan.

      That’s all you need for a first session. The sections below are there when you want more.

      Before the session

      Before each session

      • Try the activity yourself. Cut or prepare anything that slows building.
      • Put materials into one set per team.
      • Read the Bible passage and notes, and answer the three questions yourself first.
      • Check who your safeguarding lead is.
      • Make sure consent and health forms are in for every young person.

      Your role

      • Guide, don’t lecture. Answer questions with questions: “How could you find out?”
      • Let young people do most of the talking.
      • Don’t fix their designs. Let the test show what works.
      • Praise effort, teamwork, and trying again, not only winning.

      During the session

      The session flow

      Every session follows the same six steps. The times are estimates for a 90-minute session. Your group sets the pace, so adjust as you go.

      1. Connectabout 10 min

        Welcome everyone. Play a warm-up game. In the first session, agree group agreements. In later sessions, ask how last week’s Live it out step went.

      2. Introduceabout 10 min

        Share a real-world problem and the big idea. Show the key steps and the safety rules. Teams predict what will happen.

      3. Explore25–45 min, depending on the activity

        Teams build or investigate. Walk around and ask questions while they work.

      4. Improve and share10–20 min

        Teams change one thing, test again, and tell the group what they found.

      5. Reflectabout 15 min (at least 10)

        Use the Bible passage and the three reflection questions. Talk in pairs first, then as a group. Connect reflection to what actually happened.

      6. Live it out and close5–10 min

        Each person names one small action for the week. Ask for quick feedback. Clean up together and close.

      How the flow supports the learning journey

      I2I’s learning journey is Unlearn → Rediscover → Live it out → Impact. The session flow is how you walk that journey in one meeting. Connect and Introduce raise the questions. Explore and Improve give a shared experience to think about. Reflect moves through Unlearn and Rediscover. Live it out and close turns reflection into one action, and repeated actions lead to Impact over time.

      When things don’t go to plan

      • People arrive late: start Connect with whoever is there. Give late arrivals a role in an existing team rather than repeating instructions for everyone.
      • Instructions aren’t clear: stop the group, demonstrate the step again with real materials, and ask one participant to explain it back.
      • Materials are limited: use bigger teams, give each person a clear role, or prepare some parts beforehand.
      • A build fails, or results differ from predictions: treat it as data. Ask, “What happened? Why? What will you change?” Don’t fix it for them.
      • A team finishes early: offer the activity’s “Make it harder” idea, or ask them to help test another team’s design.
      • Building or discussion takes longer: shorten Improve and share to one quick change and one retest. Keep at least 10 minutes for Reflect and 5 for closing.
      • A project needs more time: pause at a clear point, store the work safely, and continue next session, starting with a short reminder of what each team was doing.

      Always protect: safety, every participant having a real role, a short reflection, and the closing. Everything else can be shortened.

      Friendly competition (optional)

      Some activities, such as the catapult target round or the balloon car distance test, can include a friendly challenge. Use clear criteria, the same materials and practice time for every team, and keep everyone taking part. No one is eliminated. Recognise improvement, careful testing, and teamwork as well as results. Never score faith, personal sharing, or spiritual growth. The community showcase is never a competition.

      The session steps are adapted from the UNICEF Adolescent Kit guide “Eight session steps,” which recommends a routine set of steps adapted to each group. UNICEF does not endorse this toolkit.

      Starting well

      Young people answer honestly only when they feel safe. Build trust before asking personal questions. Start with a game that needs everyone; the Warm-ups page has ideas.

      Group agreements

      Agree on these in the first session, in the young person’s own words if possible. Remind the group each time.

      • You can always pass. No one has to answer.
      • What others share stays here. Don’t repeat someone’s story outside the group.
      • Listen without laughing or fixing.
      • Speak for yourself. Share your own story, not someone else’s.

      Be honest about one limit. Say it at the start: “If someone tells us they are being hurt or are unsafe, we will tell someone who can help.” Then no one is surprised later.

      Team roles

      Give each person a role, and switch roles each session so everyone gets to lead.

      RoleWhat they do
      DirectorLeads the design and guides builders with words or gestures. A strong role for a young person who can’t easily handle materials.
      BuilderHandles materials and puts the design together (everyone helps).
      TesterRuns each test fairly and counts or measures the result.
      RecorderRemembers, draws, or writes down predictions and results.
      PresenterShares the team’s design and learning.

      Talking about failure

      How you respond when a design breaks teaches more than any lesson.

      Instead of“That didn’t work.”
      Try“What did that test teach you?”
      Instead of“Here, do it like this.”
      Try“Where did it break first? Why there?”
      Instead of“Good job, you won.”
      Try“What change made the biggest difference?”

      Leading reflection

      Every session ends with the same three questions, in this order.

      UnlearnWhat did you believe about yourself while you built? Where did that thought come from?
      RediscoverWhat does God say is true about you?
      Live it outWhat will you do differently this week? Name one choice, relationship, or habit.

      The last answer matters most. “I am wonderfully made” is a good start. “This week I’ll share my idea in class instead of staying quiet” is the goal.

      Each concept suggests a different way for young people to answer, such as drawing or a pair walk, so reflection stays fresh.

      Following up

      • Tell a partner. Each young person tells a partner their Live it out plan.
      • Check in next time. If the group meets again, start with: “How did your Live it out go?”
      • Hand it to the church. After a single session, ask young people if the youth leader can hear their plan. The leader follows up within a week.
      • Grace, not shame. If someone didn’t do it, that’s not failure. Ask what got in the way, then try again, the way a team redesigns after a test.

      If a young person tells you about abuse or harm

      1. Stay calm. Listen carefully. Don’t show shock or doubt.
      2. Let them speak at their own pace, in their own words. Don’t ask leading questions or investigate.
      3. Tell them they were right to tell you, and that it is not their fault.
      4. Don’t promise to keep it secret. Explain kindly that you need to tell someone who can help keep them safe.
      5. Write down what they said, in their words, with the date and time.
      6. Tell your church’s safeguarding lead the same day, and follow your church’s policy. Don’t confront the person they named.

      Include everyone in the room

      NeedWhat helps
      Limited movement or hand useA space everyone can reach. Work at a comfortable height. Bigger materials. Offer the Director role.
      Low vision or blindLet them hold materials and a finished example first. Use tests they can hear or feel. Describe results out loud.

      Using a phone (optional)

      Every activity works with no electricity, internet, or devices. A few activities include a short “Have a phone? Try this” idea, such as filming in slow motion or timing with a stopwatch. Use the phone only after building, so building stays hands-on.

      Safety basics

      • Adults use knives and cut bottles or thick cardboard. Young people share scissors carefully.
      • Check the space for hazards before young people arrive.
      • Ask young people and families privately about health needs, such as seizures, allergies, or medicine. Keep this private.
      • Have clean drinking water and a basic first-aid kit nearby.
      • Ask young people and families before taking photos or videos. Never post faces online without permission.

        Rediscover

        Wonderfully made

        You were designed with more care than any engineer can copy.

        Usually about 90 minutes. Adjust to your group.

        LEARNING GOALS

        Identity goalYoung people can say that God made them with care, and name one way that changes how they treat themselves or others.
        STEM goalYoung people can explain how engineers copy ideas from the human body, and why that is hard to do.
        Big idea (2 minutes)“How we see ourselves shapes how we build and how we live. Today we’ll study one of the best-designed things in this room: you.”

        ACTIVITY CHOICES

        • Robotic hand: best for groups that enjoy building.
        • Pinhole eye: best for sunny days and quick builds.
        • Fingerprint detectives: best for very low-cost settings and short time.

        Bible passage: Psalm 139:13–14

        What it meansDavid praises God for making him with care, even before he was born. Every person is made on purpose and fully known by God.
        What it doesn’t meanIt isn’t about looks or skills, and no body is better than another. It praises God; it isn’t about being proud of ourselves.
        UnlearnWhat have you believed about your body, your looks, or your abilities that isn’t true?

        Robotic hand

        Best for: groups that enjoy building.

        About 45 min of activity · prep about 10 min · low cost · indoorNo reading neededMedium energyLow costIndoorScienceEngineering
        Download this theme pack

        Why this activity: Young people study their own tendons, then discover how hard it is to copy even one finger.

        STEM: Science: how tendons pull fingers to bend them; Engineering: designing a finger that bends and grips.

        Prep (estimate): About 10 minutes. Gather materials and try one finger yourself.

        Materials per team

        A piece of thin cardboard (a cereal box works), 5 plastic drinking straws, about 1 m of string or yarn, tape, scissors to share, and small things to pick up, like bottle caps or paper balls.

        Can’t find it? What matters: the card must fold cleanly and spring back a little, the guides must keep each string close to the finger, and the string must slide easily. Rolled paper tubes taped flat can replace straw pieces. Yarn, thread, or fishing line can replace string. Any thin card, such as a cereal box or folder, works. These swaps are our suggestions and haven’t been tested, so try one first.

        How much to prepare

        ItemTotal

        Amounts are estimates based on the materials list. Prepare a few spares.

        Steps
        1. Everyone wiggles their fingers and watches the tendons (the cords that pull the fingers) move on the back of the hand.
        2. Trace around one person’s hand and wrist on the cardboard, then cut it out.
        3. Draw a line across each finger where the knuckles bend: three lines on each finger, two on the thumb. Fold the cardboard back and forth along each line so it bends easily.
        4. Cut the straws into short pieces, about 2 cm long. Tape one piece flat on each finger section between the fold lines. Don’t cover the folds, or the finger won’t bend.
        5. Each team member takes one finger. Thread a piece of string from the fingertip down through that finger’s straw pieces and out past the wrist. Tape the string firmly to the fingertip.
        6. Predict which finger will be hardest to control. Then hold the wrist flat and pull one string at a time; the finger should curl.
        7. Test: pick up three objects using only the robotic hand. Then redesign straw spacing, fold positions, or string length, and test again.

        Best for: sunny days and quick builds.

        About 30 min of activity · prep about 10 min · free · indoor or outdoorNo reading neededLow energyFreeIndoor or outdoorScienceTechnology

        Why this activity: Young people build a simple model of one part of the eye: light passing through a small opening makes an upside-down picture on a screen.

        ItemTotal

        Amounts are estimates based on the materials list. Prepare a few spares.

        Steps
        1. On the first card, draw a line 85 mm from one short edge. On the second card, draw a line 70 mm from one short edge. Put double-sided tape along each line.
        2. Roll each card into a tube, from the edge without tape, until the other edge reaches the tape line. Press the edges together. You now have one larger tube and one smaller tube. The smaller tube should slide inside the larger one: snug, but able to move.

        Bridge to reflection: Your viewer made a dim, upside-down picture. Your real eye does far more: it adjusts to light and works with your brain all the time without you thinking about it.

        The science: Light travels in straight lines. Light from the top of an object passes through the pinhole and lands low on the screen, and light from the bottom lands high, so the picture is upside down. A larger distance makes a larger but dimmer picture. Your eye also forms an upside-down image on the retina, the light-sensitive layer at the back. Your brain processes the signals so you see the world the right way. This viewer models only some parts of the eye: it has no lens, and it cannot adjust itself.

Young people who are blind or have low vision can measure and roll the tubes, keep the record, and ask others to describe the picture. If several participants can’t see the result, choose the robotic hand or fingerprint activity instead.

        Catapult

        Best for: target challenges.

        About 40 min of activity · prep about 5 min · low cost · indoorNo reading neededMedium energyLow costIndoorScienceEngineeringMath
        Download this theme pack

        Why this activity: When you press the arm down, the stick bends and the rubber bands stretch. That stored energy becomes motion when you let go.

        STEM: Science: stored energy turning into motion; Engineering: adjusting the design to change distance; Math: measuring distances and comparing results.

        Prep (estimate): About 5 minutes. Count out sticks and rubber bands for each team.

        Materials per team

        8 popsicle (craft) sticks, 5 rubber bands, a plastic bottle cap, glue or strong tape, soft things to launch such as paper balls, and a target cup 2 m away.

        Can’t find it? What matters: the arm must bend and spring back, and the bands must grip tightly. Hair ties can replace rubber bands. A plastic spoon or the bottom of a paper cup can hold the paper ball instead of a bottle cap. These swaps are our suggestions and haven’t been tested.

        How much to prepare

        ItemTotal

        Amounts are estimates based on the materials list. Prepare a few spares.

        Steps
        1. Pull a rubber band back and hold it: “What is this storing? What happens if I never let go?”
        2. Stack 6 sticks neatly. Wrap one rubber band tightly around each end of the stack. This is the stack.
        3. Lay a seventh stick across the middle of the stack, at a right angle, to make a cross. Hold it in place with one or two rubber bands crossed in an X. This is the launching arm.
        4. Lay the eighth stick under the stack, lined up with the launching arm. This is the base.
        5. Wrap a rubber band around one end of the base and the same end of the arm. Now the base and arm form a V lying on its side, with the stack between them.
        6. Glue or tape the bottle cap, open side up, onto the raised end of the arm. If you use glue, let it dry.
        7. To launch: hold the base flat on the table with one hand. Put a paper ball in the cap. Press the cap down with your other hand, then let go. Point it away from people.
        8. Retrieval: launch only when the area is clear. Everyone collects paper balls only when the facilitator says all launching has stopped.
        9. Predict what will change the result most, such as the position of the stack or how far you press the arm.
        10. Test: launch three times at the same setting. Measure the distance from the front of the base to where each ball first lands, or count hits on the target. Then change one thing only, and launch three times again. Compare averages or hit counts.

        If it doesn’t work: If the arm doesn’t spring back, check that the bands are tight and the stack hasn’t slipped. If balls fall out of the cap, press the arm more slowly before letting go. Try moving the stack along the arm and compare results.

        Ask while they work: What are we keeping the same? Did three launches give the same result? What one change will you test next?

        Safety: Never aim at people. Launch soft objects only. Collect objects only when everyone has stopped.

        Science, inclusion, and extensions

        Bridge to reflection: The arm held energy the whole time you pressed it, but nothing moved until you let go.

        The science: Pressing the arm bends the wooden stick and stretches the rubber bands. Both store elastic energy. When you let go, that energy becomes motion in the arm and the ball. The stack is the pivot (the point the arm turns around). Moving it changes the angle and how the arm bends.

        Include everyone: Pre-bundle sticks for young people with limited hand use; they can aim and launch. A tin can as the target lets blind young people hear a hit.

        Community design project

        Best for: the final session of a series.

        About 40 min of activity · prep about 5 min · low cost · indoor or outdoorSome readingMedium energyLow costIndoor or outdoorEngineering
        Download this theme pack

        Why this activity: Young people choose a real local problem, design a first step, and share it publicly.

        STEM: Engineering: the design process: understand a need, sketch or model an idea, get feedback, and improve it. Drawing and presenting help teams share their ideas clearly.

        Prep (estimate): About 10 minutes. Gather large paper or flattened cardboard, drawing materials, and a box of clean scrap materials. Decide who will follow up with teams after the session.

        Materials per team

        Large paper or flattened cardboard (one per team), markers, pencils, or charcoal, tape, scissors, and clean scrap materials for models, such as small boxes, bottle caps, paper cups, card, string, and straws. Collect a mixed box for the whole group; teams take what their design needs.

        Can’t find it? If paper is short, draw the community map with chalk on the ground or with a stick in sand outside, and photograph or copy it afterwards. Models can be made from whatever clean scrap is available.

        How much to prepare

        ItemTotal

        Amounts are estimates based on the materials list. Prepare a few spares.

        Steps
        1. Draw a simple map of your community together on large paper, cardboard, or the ground. Mark homes, roads, the church, schools, water points, and places people gather.
        2. Participants mark places where people face problems, such as unsafe crossings, rubbish, a lack of light, or loneliness.
        3. Each team picks one problem and answers: Who is affected? What might cause it? What do we not yet know?
        4. Check understanding: where practical, teams ask someone affected, a family member, or a leader whether they have understood the problem and whether their idea would help. Teams don’t assume they already know what others need.
        5. Teams make a poster or a prototype. A prototype is a simple first model, made quickly to test an idea. Show the problem, who it affects, the idea, and one first small step.
        6. Pitch: each team explains its idea to another team in about two minutes. The listening team asks, “What would make this work better?” Teams improve their design.
        7. Display: teams set out their posters or models. Half of each team stays to explain while the other half visits other teams; then they swap.
        8. Take it further: each team names one person who could help, such as a pastor, school leader, or local official, and plans how to share the idea.

        If it doesn’t work: If a team can’t choose a problem, ask them to choose the one they talked about most. If an idea is too big, ask: “What is one small first step you could take this month?”

        Ask while they work: Who did you ask about this problem? What don’t we know yet? What is one small first step?

        Optional showcase: Invite families and church members to a gallery walk where teams share their ideas. Celebrate every team. This isn’t a contest, so there are no prizes or rankings. Keep costs low, for example with a shared meal. Certificates of recognition are in the Forms section.

        Follow-up: Before the session, agree who will contact each team afterwards and when, such as a local youth leader two weeks later. If you are a visiting facilitator, hand this to a local leader before you leave.

        Science, inclusion, and extensions

        Bridge to reflection: You started with a real need and a first small step. That’s how impact begins.

        Make it harder (optional): Build a simple working prototype. Show it to at least two people affected by the problem, record their feedback, and improve the prototype using what they said.

        Include everyone: Young people can present by speaking, through a teammate, with a model, or with drawings.

        Source: adapted from the UNICEF Adolescent Kit activity guides “Forming problem and opportunity statements,” “Prototyping,” and “Organizing an exhibition.” Prep times, swaps, calculator amounts, and any changes to the steps are our own and haven’t been tested yet.

        Bottle light

        Best for: sunny days or a strong torch, and a real-world invention.

        About 30 min of activity · prep about 15 min · free · outdoorNo reading neededLow energyFreeOutdoorScienceTechnologyEngineering
        Download this theme pack

        Why this activity: A water-filled bottle in a roof can bring daylight into a dark room without electricity. Teams test how it changes the light inside a model house.

        STEM: Science: water bends and spreads light; Technology: a real low-cost lighting technology used in homes; Engineering: fitting and sealing the bottle.

        Prep (estimate): About 20 minutes. Cut the box openings and test the setup once with your torch.

        Materials per team

        A large cardboard box for the model house, a clear 1–2 litre plastic bottle with its cap, plain water, a 15 cm square of card for the collar, scissors (adult), tape, and a torch. Optional: a dark cloth.

        Can’t find it? What matters: the bottle must be clear and smooth, the room must be dark inside, and the light source must stay in the same place for each comparison. Any clear plastic bottle can work. A crate covered with a dark cloth can replace a box. Bright sunlight from a window can replace the torch if it stays steady. These swaps are our suggestions and haven’t been tested.

        How much to prepare

        ItemTotal

        Amounts are estimates based on the materials list. Prepare a few spares.

        Steps
        1. Adult preparation: cut a hole in the roof (top) of the box, just big enough for the bottle to pass through. Cut a small window in one side so you can look in.
        2. Make the collar: draw a circle in the centre of the card square, the same size as the bottle. Cut it out. Make short cuts outward from the hole all around, and bend the small flaps up.
        3. Slide the collar over the bottle so it rests about one third of the way down from the cap. The collar will hold the bottle in the roof hole.
        4. Comparison 1, open hole: with no bottle in the roof, shine the torch straight down at the roof hole from the same height each time. Look through the side window and describe the light.
        5. Comparison 2, empty bottle: put the empty bottle (with its cap on) into the roof hole so the collar rests on the roof. The top part sticks out above the roof, and most of the bottle hangs inside. Tape or cover any gaps around the collar. Shine the torch the same way and look again.
        6. Comparison 3, water bottle: remove the bottle, fill it with water, put the cap back on, and put it back the same way. Shine the torch the same way and look again.
        7. Repeat each comparison once more in the same order. Record what you see each time: where the light falls, and how far it spreads.
        8. Discuss: what did the water change?

        If it doesn’t work: If it’s hard to see any difference, darken the room or cover the box with a cloth, and keep the torch at exactly the same position and angle.

        Ask while they work: Where does the light fall with the open hole? What changed with water? Where does the light come from?

        Safety: Use plain water only. An adult does the cutting. A real roof installation needs the owner’s permission and a skilled installer.

        Science, inclusion, and extensions

        Bridge to reflection: The bottle didn’t make any light. It took light that was already shining and spread it into a dark space where people could use it.

        The science: Light changes direction when it passes from air into water and back out. This is called refraction. The water-filled bottle redirects incoming light and spreads it inside the room. It does not create or store light, so a real bottle roof light works only in daylight. Real installations seal the bottle into a roof.

        Make it harder (optional): Choose one feature to change, such as how far the bottle hangs into the room or the bottle’s size, and keep everything else the same. Predict, then compare the light pattern twice for each version. Explain your results using refraction.

        Include everyone: Participants who are blind or have low vision can measure and cut the collar with support, place the bottle, hold the torch in position, and keep the record. Teammates describe exactly where the light falls each time, using agreed words such as “only under the bottle” or “across the floor.”

        Source: adapted from Science in School, “Light refraction in primary education: the solar bottle bulb” (Wegner and Ohlberger), based on Alfredo Moser’s design. Our changes: the three-way comparison order. Prep times, swaps, calculator amounts, and any changes to the steps are our own and haven’t been tested yet.

        Warm-ups

        Short games to build trust at the start, wake people up in the middle, or calm the group after a serious talk. Use them whenever the group needs to connect.

        Human Knot

        10 min, high energy, start of a session

        Stand in a circle. Everyone reaches across and takes two different people’s hands, then the group untangles itself without letting go.

        Include everyone: If anyone can’t take part physically, play Group Story instead.

        Name and motion

        5 min, medium energy, first session

        Each person says their name with a motion. The whole group repeats both. Go around the circle.

        Include everyone: A motion can be anything: a nod, a blink, a wave.

        Stand up if…

        5 min, low to medium energy, start of a session

        Read statements such as “Stand up if you’ve ever fixed something that was broken” or “…if you speak more than one language.” Young people discover what they share.

        Include everyone: Raising a hand works as well as standing.

        Human machine

        5 min, medium energy, before a build

        One person starts a repeating motion and sound. Others join one at a time, each connecting to someone already moving, until the group becomes one machine.

        Why it helps: Every part matters to the whole machine.

        Count to 20

        5 min, low energy, calming

        The group counts aloud, one person at a time, in no set order. If two people speak at once, start again from 1.

        Include everyone: For deaf young people, play by raising fingers instead of speaking.

        Back-to-back drawing

        10 min, low energy, middle of a session

        Pairs sit back to back. One describes a simple shape without naming it; the other draws it. Compare, then swap.

        Why it helps: Clear communication is a skill engineers depend on.

        Three-minute tower

        5 min, high energy, before a build

        Teams get 10 sheets of paper and no tape. Build the tallest standing tower in three minutes.

        Why it helps: A fast first try, and a reminder that first tries are meant to be improved.

        Same and different

        5 min, low energy, first session

        Pairs find three things they have in common and one way they’re different.

        Mirror

        5 min, low energy, after a heavy reflection

        Pairs face each other. One moves slowly; the other mirrors. Swap leaders when the facilitator says so.

        Strength web

        10 min, low energy, closing

        Stand in a circle. Holding the end of a ball of string, name a strength you saw in someone, then pass the ball to them. Keep going until everyone is part of the web.

        Why it helps: Each person is seen and connected.

        Group story

        5 min, low energy, any time

        The group tells one story, each person adding a single sentence. Everyone can take part from where they sit.

        Local notes

        The rest of the toolkit works anywhere. Local notes add details for a specific country: contacts, language, local examples, and what earlier groups learned.

        Liberia

        Where Identity to Impact began.

        The first program

        Identity to Impact began as a six-week Saturday STEM bootcamp at the Liberian Learning Center, February to April 2026. It was planned for 20 teens and grew to 26 (14 boys and 12 girls, ages 13–19). Attendance averaged 86%.

        Teens built water filters, drop protectors, balloon cars, robotic hands, and catapults. Each session had a short talk about identity and growth, a hands-on build, and reflection. The program ended with an Innovation Showcase, where teams presented posters on problems in Liberia in a gallery walk for families and guests.

        That program ran in a secular setting, where identity meant confidence and growth. This toolkit adds the faith-based identity framework for church settings.

        What young people said

        “This program helped me to be more confident in myself.”Participant
        “The program allowed bonding, creativity and knowledge to flow.”Participant

        Young people rated themselves each week on a 4-point scale. Between 18 and 21 of the 26 teens filled in the sheet each week. Average confidence went from 3.09 in week 1 to 3.33 in week 5. Leadership went from 2.48 to 3.00.

        What we learned

        What happenedWhat changed in the toolkit
        Some builds felt rushed, even in 3-hour sessionsActivities sized for 90 minutes, with tips to prepare materials ahead
        Young people asked for more time to bondGroup agreements, trust games, and the Warm-ups page
        The final event cost more than three times as much as all activity materialsAdvice to keep events simple
        Before-and-after surveys were hard to compareMatching start and end identity checks, plus observation forms
        Self-scores started high, leaving little room to show growthReporting real numbers, and the Live it out tracker as stronger evidence

        Training

        The goal isn’t one great facilitator. It’s a chain of facilitators who each train the next: one domino at a time.

        Start with yourself

        Facilitators lead best when they have done the three steps themselves. Before leading young people, take 30 minutes alone or with a mentor.

        UnlearnWhich voices have most shaped how you see yourself: people’s opinions, culture, past experiences, successes, or failures? Which don’t match what God says?
        RediscoverRead Psalm 139:13–16 and Romans 12:1–2. What does God say is true about you?
        Live it outWhat is one thing you’ll do differently this month because of it? Who will you tell?

        A facilitator training session

        About 3 hours, for a group of new facilitators.

        PartTimeWhat happens
        Welcome and framework30 minThe big idea, the three steps, and the four rules that protect young people
        Personal walkthrough30 minThe “Start with yourself” questions above, in pairs
        Experience a session90 minNew facilitators take part in a full session as participants
        Safeguarding practice20 minPractice what to do if a young person shares harm, using the six steps
        Plan a first session20 minChoose a concept and activity, and fill in a session plan

        The handoff path

        Tick items as you go. Your progress is saved on this device.

        1. Watch

        The new facilitator joins a full session as a helper and observer.

        2. Co-lead

        They lead two or three steps while the experienced facilitator leads the rest.

        3. Lead

        They run a full session. The experienced facilitator watches, using the observation form, and steps in only for safety.

        4. Teach

        They take someone else through Watch, Co-lead, and Lead.

        Optional: video examples for training

        With permission, film short clips of a skilled facilitator leading the big idea, handling a failed test, and leading Reflect. New facilitators can watch the hardest parts again, even offline.

        Optional: remote coaching

        After a new facilitator’s first session on their own, talk through the after-session questions together on a 20-minute video call. This way one experienced facilitator can support several communities.

        Testing the toolkit

        The key question: can someone who didn’t design the program understand and run it?

        1. Give a new facilitator this toolkit and one week to prepare one concept and activity of their choice.
        2. Watch them run it, using the facilitator observation form.
        3. Afterward, ask: What was clear? What was confusing? What was missing?
        4. Revise the toolkit, then test with the next facilitator.

        Missions-in-a-Box, later

        Short-term mission teams with STEM skills can serve alongside a local church using this same handoff path. Their role is to co-lead and equip, not to own the program. Success is when the local church runs it after the team leaves.
